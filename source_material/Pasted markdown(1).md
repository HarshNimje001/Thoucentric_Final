**STRATEGIC QUESTION**

**Which single investment best balances demand, network performance, customer expectations and future readiness to build a future-ready charging network by 2030?**

2.     Root Cause Analysis (Using 5 Whys)

**Why is there underutilization in the first place?**
 → Some stations may have capacity exceeding public-charging demand in their immediate catchment.

**Why are only a few metropolitan areas congested if 68% prefer home charging?**
 → Public-charging demand is concentrated among specific customer segments and locations rather than evenly distributed across EV owners.

**If commercial vehicles rely on public charging, why aren't highway/outskirts stations seeing the same demand?**
 → Commercial charging demand depends on actual operating routes and charging behaviour, which may not align with EIC's highway/semi-urban locations.

**Was EIC's network planned according to regional and area-wise demand?**
 → If location and capacity decisions were not sufficiently granular to reflect area-level demand and customer usage patterns, capacity could have been allocated away from emerging demand centres.

**Why would this mismatch persist?**
 → Because charging infrastructure is fixed and difficult to relocate or resize, while demand changes across regions, customer segments and peak periods; without continuous demand-led planning, initial allocation errors can persist.

**ROOT CAUSE**

**A potential gap in the granularity and responsiveness of EIC's network planning and capacity-allocation process, resulting in fixed charging capacity not being sufficiently aligned with evolving area-level, customer-segment and peak-period demand.**

** **

**STRATEGIC IMPLICATION**

**The investment should therefore address the underlying demand–capacity mismatch rather than assume that simply adding more charging infrastructure will solve the problem.**

**STEP 3 — STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATION**

**3.1 Strategic Decision Objective**

EIC operates 2,800 public charging stations with an average utilization of 46%. While major metropolitan stations experience peak-hour congestion, several highway and semi-urban stations operate below capacity. At the same time, EV registrations are expected to grow by 28–35% annually, increasing the need for a future-ready charging network.

Our root-cause analysis identifies a potential **gap in the granularity and responsiveness of EIC's network planning and capacity-allocation process**, resulting in fixed charging capacity not being sufficiently aligned with evolving area-level, customer-segment and peak-period demand.

Therefore, the strategic question is:

**Which investment best addresses EIC's underlying network planning and capacity-allocation gap while delivering customer value, financial viability, operational efficiency and long-term readiness for 2030?**

EIC can implement only **one** of the three proposed investments in the current cycle; therefore, the opportunity cost of the decision must also be considered.

---

**3.2 Analytical Assumptions**

The case provides the major market facts and investment alternatives but does not provide sufficient granular information to conclusively explain the underlying demand mismatch. The following assumptions therefore bridge only the critical analytical gaps required for decision-making.

**Assumption 1 — EV growth is concentrated in urban markets**

The case indicates approximately **32% annual EV growth**, while metropolitan stations experience peak-hour congestion and several highway and semi-urban stations operate below capacity.

**We assume that the projected 32% annual EV growth is not evenly distributed across EIC's urban, semi-urban and highway catchments, with a relatively higher concentration of incremental EV demand in metropolitan/urban markets.**

**Rationale:** If EV growth were evenly distributed, charging demand would be expected to increase more consistently across EIC's network. The coexistence of metropolitan congestion and underutilization elsewhere suggests that the **location of incremental demand**, not only its overall growth rate, is important.

**Implication:** EIC should not treat 32% growth as a uniform demand multiplier across all stations. Future capacity decisions need to identify where demand is actually emerging.

---

**Assumption 2 — Public-charging demand is concentrated among specific customer segments**

The case states that **68% of EV owners prefer home charging**, while commercial fleets rely heavily on public charging.

**We assume that locations with a higher concentration of public-charging-dependent users, particularly commercial fleets and users without convenient home charging, generate disproportionately higher public-charging demand than locations with similar EV penetration but greater access to home charging.**

**Rationale:** EV ownership does not directly translate into public-charging demand. A location can have significant EV adoption but relatively low public-charging demand if most users charge at home, while another location can generate high public-charging demand because a larger share of users depends on public infrastructure.

**Implication:** EIC should plan around **public-charging demand**, rather than using EV penetration alone to determine station location and capacity.

---

**Assumption 3 — Private operators are setting a higher service benchmark**

The case states that private charging operators are increasing competition and raising customer expectations around **accessibility, reliability and charging speed**.

**We assume that private operators are improving these service dimensions sufficiently to create a higher customer benchmark against which EIC's charging experience is increasingly evaluated.**

**Rationale:** The case establishes increased competition and rising expectations but does not explicitly quantify the service gap between EIC and private operators. This assumption provides the mechanism connecting competitive entry with increasing customer dissatisfaction.

**Implication:** The selected investment must improve not only infrastructure capacity but also the customer experience, particularly **availability, charging speed and reliability**.

---

**Assumption 4 — Existing charging capacity is difficult to reallocate after deployment**

EIC already operates 2,800 stations distributed across major cities and national highways.

**We assume that charging infrastructure is sufficiently location-specific and capital-intensive that relocating or materially resizing an existing station is significantly less practical than improving future location and capacity decisions before new capital is deployed.**

**Rationale:** If capacity could be freely moved between locations, underutilization and congestion could be corrected through simple reallocation. The location-specific nature of charging infrastructure means that a demand-capacity mismatch can persist once infrastructure has been deployed.

**Implication:** EIC needs to improve the **quality and responsiveness of its network planning and capacity-allocation process**, rather than relying only on additional infrastructure.

---

**3.3 Strategic Requirements Derived from the Assumptions**

The assumptions establish five requirements for the selected investment:

1. **Improve demand-to-capacity      alignment** rather      than simply increase total infrastructure. 
2. **Account for location-level      differences** in      public-charging demand. 
3. **Improve utilization of the existing      network**, given the      current 46% average utilization. 
4. **Improve customer experience**, particularly availability,      charging speed and reliability. 
5. **Remain scalable through 2030** as EV demand continues to grow. 

These requirements form the basis for evaluating the three investment options.

**3.5 Option A — Nationwide Fast Charging Expansion**

**Investment:** ₹4,800 Cr
 **Implementation:** 4 years
 **Network growth:** +70%
 **Additional infrastructure:** 2,000 DC fast-charging stations

**Root-Cause Resolution — 2/5**

Option A directly increases physical charging capacity. However, EIC already has 2,800 stations, with 46% average utilization and simultaneous congestion and underutilization across locations.

Therefore, adding 2,000 stations does not directly solve the underlying question:

**Where should additional capacity be deployed to match actual and future demand?**

A can address the problem if the additional stations are accurately located in high-demand areas, but this requires better demand-location understanding first.

**Market Demand Alignment — 3/5**

A is supported by strong EV growth, but the assumed uneven distribution of growth means that applying the same expansion logic across the network could create further capacity-demand mismatches.

**Customer Impact — 4/5**

Additional fast-charging capacity can improve availability, reduce congestion and improve charging speed where demand genuinely exceeds capacity.

**Financial Viability — 2/5**

At ₹4,800 Cr, A consumes approximately **96% of the ₹5,000 Cr annual capital budget**, leaving limited financial flexibility.

**ROI / Value Creation — 2/5**

The case does not provide expected utilization, incremental revenue or operating costs for the additional stations. Therefore, the financial return cannot be established quantitatively.

**Operational Efficiency — 3/5**

A can reduce congestion through additional capacity but does not directly improve utilization of existing underperforming stations.

**Scalability to 2030 — 5/5**

A provides the largest physical expansion at +70% network growth and therefore offers significant capacity for future EV demand.

**Technology Readiness — 5/5**

The option primarily involves deployment of established fast-charging infrastructure and does not require the same level of digital transformation as Option C.

**Environmental Sustainability — 3/5**

Additional charging infrastructure supports EV adoption, but renewable-energy integration is not a central component of the option.

**Implementation Risk — 3/5**

Deploying 2,000 additional stations involves significant site-selection, grid-connectivity, construction, maintenance and network-integration requirements.

**Strategic Assessment**

**Option A is strongest if EIC's primary problem is insufficient physical charging capacity. However, our root-cause analysis indicates that the more fundamental issue is the alignment of capacity with demand. A increases the network's physical size without directly improving the mechanism used to determine where that capacity should be deployed.**

**3.6 Option B — Rural & Tier-2 EV Accessibility**

**Investment:** ₹3,900 Cr
 **Implementation:** 5 years
 **Geographic coverage:** +55%

**Root-Cause Resolution — 2/5**

B primarily addresses geographic accessibility. However, our root cause concerns the alignment of charging capacity with actual demand.

Expanding the network does not automatically improve EIC's ability to identify where public-charging demand will emerge.

**Market Demand Alignment — 3/5**

B has strong long-term market-development potential, but demand across new locations is uncertain. The case does not establish that every target location will develop sufficient public-charging demand within the investment horizon.

**Customer Impact — 4/5**

B can significantly improve charging accessibility outside major metropolitan areas and support broader EV adoption.

**Financial Viability — 3/5**

₹3,900 Cr represents approximately **78% of the ₹5,000 Cr annual capital budget**. This is less capital-intensive than A but still represents a substantial commitment.

**ROI / Value Creation — 3/5**

The expected +55% geographic coverage provides a measurable strategic outcome, but the case does not provide utilization or revenue projections for the additional locations.

**Operational Efficiency — 3/5**

B expands network reach but does not directly improve utilization of EIC's existing 2,800 stations.

**Scalability to 2030 — 4/5**

The expanded geographic footprint supports long-term EV ecosystem development, although the five-year implementation timeline extends close to the 2030 horizon.

**Technology Readiness — 4/5**

The partnership-based model with fuel stations and local businesses can leverage existing locations, although distributed partnerships can increase coordination requirements.

**Environmental Sustainability — 4/5**

Broader charging access can support EV adoption and electrification across a wider geography.

**Implementation Risk — 3/5**

The five-year implementation period and dependence on multiple partners increase execution complexity.

**Strategic Assessment**

**Option B is a strong long-term accessibility and market-development strategy, but it expands EIC's physical footprint without directly solving the underlying demand-to-capacity allocation problem. It addresses where EIC operates more directly than how EIC should determine and continuously manage capacity.**

**3.7 Option C — Intelligent Charging Network Modernization**

**Investment:** ₹2,600 Cr
 **Implementation:** 3 years
 **Expected station-utilization increase:** +30%

The option includes:

- AI-enabled demand forecasting 
- Smart load balancing 
- Dynamic pricing 
- Predictive maintenance 
- Mobile reservations 
- Renewable-energy integration 
- Remote monitoring and analytics. 

**Root-Cause Resolution — 5/5**

C directly addresses the mechanism identified in the RCA.

**Demand forecasting** → identifies where and when demand is emerging.

**Remote monitoring and analytics** → identifies station-level performance.

**Smart load balancing** → improves management of available capacity.

**Dynamic pricing** → can potentially manage peak demand.

**Reservations** → improve customer visibility and planning.

**Predictive maintenance** → improves network reliability.

Therefore:

**C directly improves EIC's ability to connect demand information with network capacity and operations.**

**Market Demand Alignment — 5/5**

C is designed to respond to changing and uneven demand rather than assuming that demand will grow uniformly across EIC's network.

This directly addresses the uncertainty created by different customer segments, charging preferences and geographic demand patterns.

**Customer Impact — 5/5**

The case identifies **availability, charging speed and reliability** as the three most important customer satisfaction factors.

C addresses:

- **Availability:** demand forecasting + reservations      + monitoring 
- **Charging capacity/speed management:** smart load balancing 
- **Reliability:** predictive maintenance + remote      monitoring 

**Financial Viability — 5/5**

C requires ₹2,600 Cr, approximately **52% of the ₹5,000 Cr annual capital budget**, providing substantially greater capital flexibility than A or B.

**ROI / Value Creation — 4/5**

C targets a **30% increase in station utilization**.

Under the stated interpretation that this represents a relative increase:

**46% × 1.30 ≈ 60% utilization**

This indicates an attempt to create greater value from EIC's existing asset base. However, conventional ROI cannot be calculated because the case does not provide sufficient revenue and cost data.

**Operational Efficiency — 5/5**

C directly improves network operations through load balancing, monitoring, predictive maintenance, analytics and demand forecasting.

It therefore addresses both sides of the current problem:

**Underutilized capacity + peak congestion.**

**Scalability to 2030 — 5/5**

As EV demand continues to grow, C provides an adaptive capability that can continuously update demand forecasts and improve future capacity decisions.

**Technology Readiness — 3/5**

C has the highest technology dependency.

Successful implementation requires:

- adequate data, 
- system integration, 
- technology capability, 
- employee adoption, 
- change management. 

The case does not provide sufficient information about EIC's current digital maturity, so this remains a material execution risk.

**Environmental Sustainability — 5/5**

C explicitly includes renewable-energy integration, directly aligning with the case's sustainability priorities.

**Implementation Risk — 4/5**

C has the shortest implementation period at three years, but its digital transformation requirements create technology and change-management risks.

**Strategic Assessment**

**Option C has the strongest direct fit with the identified root cause because it improves EIC's ability to forecast, monitor and manage demand against existing network capacity. Its primary weakness is the technology and implementation dependency, which can be managed through phased deployment.**

**3.10 Strategic Recommendation**

**RECOMMEND OPTION C — INTELLIGENT CHARGING NETWORK MODERNIZATION**

**EIC should prioritize Intelligent Charging Network Modernization because its central challenge is not simply insufficient charging infrastructure, but a potential mismatch between fixed network capacity and evolving public-charging demand across locations, customer segments and peak periods. Our assumptions indicate that EV growth is likely to be uneven across EIC's operating environments, that public-charging demand is disproportionately influenced by customer segments dependent on public infrastructure, and that existing charging capacity is difficult to reallocate after deployment. Option C directly addresses this challenge through AI-enabled demand forecasting, analytics, smart load balancing, predictive maintenance and reservations. At ₹2,600 Cr, it requires the lowest investment among the three alternatives while targeting a 30% increase in station utilization and improving availability, reliability and network responsiveness.**

**3.14 Final Strategic Conclusion**

**EIC should select Option C — Intelligent Charging Network Modernization. The decision is driven primarily by strategic fit rather than cost. EIC's simultaneous congestion and underutilization indicate that simply increasing the total number of charging stations may not resolve the underlying demand-to-capacity mismatch. Assuming EV growth is unevenly distributed across EIC's operating environments, public-charging demand is disproportionately influenced by specific customer segments, and existing charging infrastructure is difficult to reallocate after deployment, EIC needs greater ability to forecast, monitor and respond to demand. Option C directly builds this capability while improving utilization, availability and reliability, requires ₹2,600 Cr compared with ₹4,800 Cr for A and ₹3,900 Cr for B, and has the shortest three-year implementation period. Most importantly, it creates the intelligence required to make future physical expansion decisions more accurately. Therefore, EIC should modernize its existing network first and use the resulting demand intelligence to guide targeted physical expansion in the subsequent investment cycle.**

**Final Recommendation**

**MODERNIZE FIRST. EXPAND WHERE THE DATA PROVES EXPANSION IS REQUIRED.**

**5.3 Strategic Business Impact**

**1. Better utilization of existing infrastructure**

The primary near-term impact is improved utilization of EIC's existing network. Rather than immediately committing substantial capital to additional infrastructure, C seeks to improve the productivity of the 2,800 stations already deployed.

**2. Improved customer experience**

Improved availability, reliability and demand management should address the three factors identified by the case as the most important drivers of customer satisfaction.

**3. Improved operational reliability**

Remote monitoring and predictive maintenance can help EIC identify operational issues earlier, reducing avoidable downtime and improving charger availability.

**4. Greater capital efficiency**

At ₹2,600 Cr, Option C requires substantially less capital than Option A (₹4,800 Cr) and Option B (₹3,900 Cr). This allows EIC to improve its existing asset base while preserving greater financial flexibility.

**5. Stronger long-term decision-making**

The most important strategic benefit is the creation of a **data-driven network planning capability**. Better demand information can help EIC determine where additional physical infrastructure is genuinely required in future investment cycles.

---

**5.4 Overall Business Impact**

**Option C can shift EIC from a predominantly infrastructure-expansion approach toward a demand-responsive network model. The immediate impact is expected to be higher utilization, lower congestion, improved availability and greater reliability. The longer-term impact is stronger capital efficiency and better evidence-based infrastructure planning, enabling EIC to make targeted expansion decisions as EV demand evolves toward 2030.**
